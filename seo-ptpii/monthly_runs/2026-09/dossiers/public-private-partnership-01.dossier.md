# Research Dossier: Public Private Partnership Co-Financing: ECAs, DFIs, Banks

*Language note: this dossier is written in English per the brief's override. Indonesian regulation and institution names are given in Indonesian with an English gloss on first use, for the writer to reuse.*

## TL;DR for the writer

- **The structural insight of this article**: the Indonesian government guarantee issued through PT Penjaminan Infrastruktur Indonesia (PT PII) sits *underneath* the capital stack — it responds to the contracting government agency's (Penanggung Jawab Proyek Kerja Sama / PJPK's) contractual performance — rather than sitting *inside* the stack as a funding tranche. Build H2 #2's mandatory table around five funding participants plus the guarantee as an explicitly non-tranche sixth row.
- **ECA eligibility is a procurement-origin question, not a sponsor-nationality question.** An export credit agency (ECA) supports export contracts tied to its own country's goods and services, bounded by a "local costs" allowance under the OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits. This is the fact foreign sponsors most often get backwards.
- **Handle PT PII's Fitch rating with care.** Cite the 12 February 2026 affirmation (BBB / F2 / AAA(idn), Outlook Stable) *by date*, alongside Fitch's 4 March 2026 revision of Indonesia's *sovereign* outlook to Negative (Bloomberg-confirmed this run). No subsequent Fitch action specific to PT PII itself was found as at this dossier's research date — do not assert a current, unqualified "Stable" outlook.
- **LKPP Regulation No. 1 of 2026 is now directly confirmed** at the primary source, with one correction to the brief: it was enacted *and* promulgated on **5 August 2026**, not 30 July 2026. Safe to cite as a genuine freshness hook with the corrected date.
- **The Riau IPP financing (ADB, IFC, SMBC, MUFG; 2019)** is a strong, dated, sourced illustration of blended co-financing mechanics — but it is a PT PLN power-purchase-agreement independent power producer (IPP), not a confirmed KPBU-structured project, and PT PII was not a party to it. Use it to illustrate the *mechanism* only; never imply PT PII involvement.

## FAQ Q&As (8 drafted, English)

### What is co-financing in an infrastructure public-private partnership?
Co-financing is when two or more lenders — typically a mix of commercial banks, export credit agencies (ECAs) and development finance institutions (DFIs) — jointly fund one project company's debt under a coordinated set of terms, rather than one institution carrying the full exposure. It differs from a co-guarantee, where multiple institutions share risk cover rather than funding; Indonesian infrastructure projects routinely need both, arranged separately.

### Which types of lenders typically fund an Indonesian PPP project?
A large Indonesian KPBU/PPP project typically draws on sponsor equity, senior commercial bank debt, an export-credit-agency-supported tranche tied to equipment or contractor sourcing, a development finance institution loan (such as from ADB or IFC, sometimes mobilizing further commercial "B loan" capital behind it), and local-currency bank debt matched to rupiah-denominated project revenue. Not every project uses all five.

### Does the Indonesian government guarantee cover the loan itself?
No. The government guarantee issued through PT Penjaminan Infrastruktur Indonesia (PT PII) responds to the contracting government agency's (PJPK's) obligations under the KPBU contract — in practice, termination payments linked to political risk, tariff-adjustment delay or land-procurement delay — not to a lender's right to be repaid. It sits underneath the financing stack rather than inside it, and it does not remove the project company's own commercial risk.

### What determines whether an export credit agency will support an Indonesian PPP project?
ECA eligibility follows the origin of the goods and services being procured, not the sponsor's nationality or the host government's identity. Under the OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits, an ECA supports export contracts tied to its own country's suppliers, with a bounded allowance for local costs incurred in the buyer's country. The procurement decision, not the nationality of capital, decides which ECA can participate.

### Can a foreign lender fund an Indonesian PPP project directly in foreign currency?
Yes, and export credit agencies, DFIs and international commercial banks regularly do. The structural problem is not legal eligibility but currency matching: many KPBU projects earn availability payments or tariffs in rupiah, while ECA- and DFI-supported tranches are typically denominated in US dollars or another hard currency. Someone in the structure — the project company, a hedge counterparty, or the lenders through pricing — carries that mismatch.

### How is co-financing different from a co-guarantee or joint risk cover?
Co-financing is about who supplies the money — banks, ECAs and DFIs jointly funding the debt tranches. A co-guarantee (or stacked guarantee/insurance cover) is about who shares the risk if something goes wrong, such as multiple institutions covering different layers of political or credit risk on the same project. A project can combine both, but they are arranged, priced and negotiated separately.

### How soon after signing must financing be in place for an Indonesian PPP project?
Transaction commentary commonly references a target of around 12 months from signing of the KPBU agreement to loan signing and initial disbursement, but this is best treated as practice convention rather than a codified statutory deadline; it was not confirmed in the text of Perpres 38/2015 or PMK 68/2024 during this review. Sponsors should confirm the specific timeline in their own PPP agreement.

### Do development finance institutions like ADB or IFC lend directly to Indonesian infrastructure, or only through intermediaries?
Both. ADB and IFC have lent directly into individual Indonesian power projects — for example, jointly financing a Sumatra gas-fired independent power producer in 2019 alongside international commercial banks — and have also funded intermediaries such as PT Indonesia Infrastructure Finance, which on-lends to a wider project pipeline. Either route can mobilize additional commercial "B loan" capital behind the DFI's own exposure.

## Edge cases & nuance

- **Co-financing ≠ co-guarantee.** August's `public-private-partnership-03` already covered guarantee-instrument comparison (MIGA/MDB/ECA cover). This article must stay confined to lending roles; if the draft starts comparing coverage terms or claim mechanics, it has drifted into the wrong article.
- **MIGA is not a co-financier.** It provides political risk insurance/credit enhancement, not loans — keep it out of the capital-stack table entirely, or mention it only to explain why it is absent from a *financing* discussion.
- **The Riau IPP is illustrative, not definitional.** It shows ADB+IFC+commercial-bank blended financing mechanics concretely, but it is a PT PLN power-purchase-agreement independent power producer, not a confirmed KPBU/PJPK contract, and PT PII was not involved. Frame it as "an example of how DFIs mobilize commercial co-lenders in Indonesian infrastructure," not as a KPBU case study.
- **A foreign sponsor's own nationality does not summon its home ECA.** What matters is where the EPC contractor and equipment suppliers are based — a sourcing decision made at the procurement stage, often before financing is even discussed.
- **The 12-month financing clock is a schedule risk multiplier, not just a filing deadline.** The more lenders in a stack, the more facility agents, conditions precedent and internal approval processes must land in the same window — this is the practical reason "more lenders" is not an unambiguous win even though it also means more available capital.
- **Currency matching has no universal fix.** Rupiah-denominated availability payments or tariffs against foreign-currency debt service is a structural feature of importing hard-currency-priced ECA/DFI debt into an IDR-revenue project — hedging, local-currency tranches, or accepting the mismatch are the three broad paths, and each has a cost that lands somewhere in the deal.
- **A single-lender domestic structure is sometimes simply the better answer** — smaller or regional projects rarely justify the intercreditor complexity of a cross-border stack; this is the natural bridge to PT PII's Pembiayaan Kreatif (Creative Financing) product for projects too small for an offshore tranche.
- **Penjaminan pemerintah ≠ subsidi, and it does not remove the BUP's commercial risk** — it covers government-side risk under the contract, not the sponsor's business risk or the lenders' credit risk on the BUP.

> Co-financing in a public-private partnership is the practice of multiple lenders — commercial banks, export credit agencies (ECAs) and development finance institutions (DFIs) — jointly funding one project company's debt under coordinated terms, rather than a single lender carrying the full exposure.
