# Research Dossier: Public Private Partnership: Indonesia vs Regional Frameworks

## TL;DR for the writer

- Three genuinely different state architectures, not three versions of the same tool. **Indonesia** = a separately incorporated, capitalised guarantee company (PT PII/BUPI) with a ring-fenced balance sheet under Perpres 78/2010. **Philippines** = statutory government guarantees under a 2023 PPP Code, backstopped by a **budget-line fund** (the PPP Risk Management Fund) administered by a government agency, not a separate company. **Vietnam** = a codified revenue-sharing **formula written directly into statute** (2020 PPP Law), with no dedicated guarantee entity or fund at all.
- All three frameworks are genuinely current and mostly post-2020, which is the article's real news hook: Vietnam's Law No. 64/2020/QH14 (effective 1 January 2021), the Philippines' RA 11966 (effective 23 December 2023, IRR effective 6 April 2024), Indonesia's PMK 68/2024 (effective 18 October 2024), and — freshest of all — **LKPP Regulation No. 1 of 2026**, enacted and effective **5 August 2026** (not 30 July, as the brief assumed — verified directly against jdih.lkpp.go.id). The 2015 ERIA ASEAN survey that SERP competitors still cite predates every one of these.
- Handle PT PII's credit rating carefully. Fitch affirmed PT PII at BBB/F2/AAA(idn), Outlook **Stable**, on **12 February 2026** — but on **4 March 2026** Fitch revised **Indonesia's sovereign outlook** from Stable to **Negative** (BBB rating affirmed), confirmed by five independent outlets. Because PT PII is rated as a Government-Related Entity credit-linked to the sovereign, the writer must not assert an unqualified "Stable" outlook for PT PII today — state the 12 Feb 2026 affirmation with its date, and do not claim it still holds without that caveat.
- Portfolio trap, specific to this English brief: do **not** pull figures from `ptpii.co.id`'s English hub — confirmed stale (33 projects, superseded RPJMN 2020–2024 figures). Use **61 projects (38 KPBU + 23 non-KPBU), >IDR 732 trillion, as at June 2026**.
- Fairness discipline is load-bearing here: no ranking language anywhere. The article's honest payoff (H2 #6) is that statutory design is a weak predictor of real outcomes compared with project-preparation quality, land acquisition, and demonstrated financial-close rates — say this plainly rather than implying any one architecture "wins."

## FAQ Q&As (8 drafted, English)

### What is a national public private partnership framework?
A national public private partnership (PPP) framework is the combination of statute, responsible institution, and support instruments a government uses to share infrastructure risk with private investors and manage the resulting contingent liabilities. Frameworks diverge even for similar projects: Indonesia relies on a separately capitalised guarantee company under Perpres No. 78 of 2010, while the Philippines (2023) and Vietnam (2020) chose different institutional architectures entirely.

### How does Indonesia's public private partnership framework compare with the Philippines' and Vietnam's?
All three states support PPP contracts, but through different architectures. Indonesia issues government guarantees through a separately capitalised state company, PT Penjaminan Infrastruktur Indonesia (Persero), under Perpres No. 78/2010. The Philippines backs statutory guarantees with a budget-funded PPP Risk Management Fund under Republic Act No. 11966 (effective 23 December 2023). Vietnam codifies a revenue-sharing formula directly in its PPP Law (2020) rather than using a guarantee entity or fund.

### What is the Philippines' PPP Risk Management Fund?
Established under Section 27 of Republic Act No. 11966 (the PPP Code, effective 23 December 2023), the PPP Risk Management Fund pays the Philippine government's contingent liabilities arising from PPP contracts. It is funded through general appropriations, income from existing PPP projects, and other sources set by the Development Budget Coordination Committee, and is administered by the PPP Center — a budget-line mechanism rather than a separately incorporated guarantee company.

### What is Vietnam's revenue-sharing mechanism under its PPP Law?
Under Article 82 of Vietnam's Law No. 64/2020/QH14 (effective 1 January 2021), if a project's actual revenue exceeds 125% of the figure in its financial plan, the investor shares 50% of the excess with the State; if revenue falls below 75% of forecast, the State covers 50% of the shortfall. A related foreign-exchange balance guarantee is capped at 30% of project revenue, a marked reduction from earlier frameworks.

### Which Southeast Asian country has the strongest public private partnership framework?
There is no single "strongest" framework — each solves a different problem. Indonesia's ring-fenced guarantee company protects the state budget from sudden claims; the Philippines' 2023 PPP Code pairs statutory guarantees with a dedicated fund; Vietnam's 2020 law gives sponsors a codified, formula-driven revenue-sharing right. Statutory design matters less than execution: project preparation quality, land acquisition speed, and demonstrated financial-close rates determine outcomes more than which framework reads strongest on paper.

### Is PT Penjaminan Infrastruktur Indonesia (PT PII) a lender to PPP projects?
No. PT PII — also known internationally as the Indonesia Infrastructure Guarantee Fund (IIGF) — is Indonesia's Badan Usaha Penjaminan Infrastruktur (BUPI), a state-owned guarantee company under the Ministry of Finance. It issues and structures government guarantees against government-side risks in KPBU/PPP contracts and supports project preparation; it does not lend money, take equity, or build or operate infrastructure. As at June 2026 its cumulative portfolio covered 61 projects worth more than IDR 732 trillion.

### Why do PPP frameworks differ so much between countries with comparable infrastructure needs?
Frameworks reflect each state's fiscal architecture and risk appetite, not just its infrastructure gap. Indonesia chose to ring-fence contingent liabilities inside a separately capitalised company so claims do not hit the budget directly. The Philippines and Vietnam instead built their support into statute and the budget process itself. Neither choice is inherently superior — a codified guarantee that needs high-level approval can, in practice, deliver less speed than one issued routinely.

### What is the legal basis for Indonesia's public private partnership guarantee scheme?
Indonesia's PPP (KPBU) guarantee framework rests on Perpres No. 38 of 2015, the primary legal basis for KPBU project delivery, and Perpres No. 78 of 2010, which established infrastructure guarantees through a Badan Usaha Penjaminan Infrastruktur (BUPI). PMK No. 68 of 2024 (effective 18 October 2024) consolidated the guarantee, viability gap funding, availability payment, and project-preparation instruments into one implementing regulation, requiring an annually published risk-allocation reference.

## Edge cases & nuance

- **The architecture difference is the article, not a generosity ranking.** Indonesia's model concentrates risk-bearing capacity in one capitalised, professionally underwritten institution; the Philippines spreads it across a statute plus a budget fund; Vietnam writes the economics directly into law with no issuing entity at all. Each trades certainty for flexibility differently — none is simply "more" or "less" supportive.
- **Statutory generosity is not the same as deal flow.** Vietnam's codified revenue-sharing right is legally strong on paper, but the FX-guarantee component specifically requires high-level (case-by-case) government approval — a friction point that a routinely issued Indonesian guarantee does not face. This is the honest counter-argument the brief's H2 #6 wants, and it is fair to both sides: Indonesia's discretionary underwriting has its own friction (case-by-case evaluation, an annual risk-allocation reference that limits what can be guaranteed).
- **Penjaminan pemerintah ≠ subsidy**, and it does not remove the badan usaha's commercial risk — PT PII's guarantee responds to government-side contractual risk, not project economics or market risk.
- **The Philippines' Fund is not a guarantee company.** It is important not to blur this: the PPP Risk Management Fund is a budget mechanism (appropriations + PPP-project income) administered by a government agency (the PPP Center), not a separately incorporated, professionally underwriting entity with its own balance sheet like PT PII. Describing it as "the Philippines' PT PII equivalent" would misstate the architecture.
- **No claim of an actual guarantee payout appears anywhere in this dossier**, for any of the three countries. Do not assert that any guarantee, fund, or revenue-sharing mechanism has ever been triggered or paid out — none of the sources gathered this run describe a specific claims history, and the brief explicitly prohibits asserting this.
- **A fourth country row was deliberately not added.** The brief permitted an optional India (VGF) or Malaysia row "only if you can source it as well as the other three." Given the WebSearch budget and the priority of getting Indonesia/Philippines/Vietnam right, this dossier follows the brief's own fallback: "three solid beats four shaky." If a future sibling article wants to add India or Malaysia, it should be sourced fresh rather than inferred from this dossier.
